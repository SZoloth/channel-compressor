## The recurring doctrine

Across the corpus, most useful advice reduces to seven ideas:

1. **Retrieval beats recognition.** Close the source and reproduce, explain, or answer before checking.
2. **Practice in the format of performance.** Use problems and past papers; mark quickly so mistakes are not rehearsed repeatedly.
3. **Make the next action specific.** Vague projects create avoidance; a visible step one lowers the cost of starting.
4. **Measure output and feedback, not hours.** A long session is not evidence of learning.
5. **Engineer the environment.** Remove the phone and other bottom-up triggers; separate deep and shallow work.
6. **Prefer systems to motivation.** Use cues, small minimum actions, and planned repetition.
7. **Stop consuming advice once the loop is defined.** More productivity content is usually a substitute for implementation.

## What can usually be skipped

- Subject- and paper-specific GCSE/A-level videos unless that exact exam is imminent.
- Additional blurting, Feynman, active-recall, “study smarter,” or note-taking videos after the core three learning videos.
- Most glow-up, lock-in, discipline, motivation, and “top 1% student” variants; they restate systems, friction, identity, and starting small.
- Cambridge routines, Q&As, college-choice videos, and vlogs unless lifestyle context is the goal.
- All six ambient study sessions for informational consumption.

## Evidence caveat

The practical protocols are often useful even when the explanation is overstated. Claims about dopamine, neural pathways, cortisol, or exact percentage improvements were not independently validated in this run; treat them as hypotheses or motivational framing, not established evidence.
